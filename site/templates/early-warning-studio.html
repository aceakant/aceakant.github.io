How it's worked out

The book. Twelve monthly batches (vintages) of {{ "2,000" }} personal loans, averaging 8,000 each, followed for 18 months on book: 30 calendar months in all. Each month some current loans miss a payment (30 days late), most often around month 7. Of those, some roll on to 60 days late, then to 90+ days, which counts as a default; the rest catch up. A default loses 75% of the balance after recoveries.

The worse batches. Batches 7, 8 and 9 were lent under looser rules: they miss payments 1.9 times as often and roll to 60 and 90+ days more often. Nothing labels them; the triggers have to find them. A downturn from month 19 lifts every batch.

Triggers. Trigger 1 checks each batch at month 3 on book, so it can flag a batch early. Triggers 2 and 3 watch the whole book; they fire at the start of the first run of 3 or more months above the line, and a shorter run is counted as a false alarm. A normal batch flagged by trigger 1 is also a false alarm.

Acting. From the month a trigger fires, collections step up and limits tighten for what it flagged. For loans in the worse batches, missed payments fall 15%, rolls to 60 days 30% and rolls to 90+ days 20%; loans that were fine improve far less (3%, 8% and 5%). It costs 4 a month for every live loan in scope, so acting on the whole book costs much more than acting on the flagged batches. "Saved" is the losses avoided minus that cost.

An illustrative sample, not any lender's real data. Real early-warning systems also use bureau data, payment behaviour, affordability and economic forecasts, and check triggers for fairness across customer groups. Built by {{ site.author }}.
