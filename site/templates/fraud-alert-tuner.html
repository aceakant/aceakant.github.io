How it's worked out

The samples. Card & bank payments: 3,000 payments a day for 14 days, about 0.5% fraudulent, split into account takeover (a fraudster in the customer's account, usually a new device and a new payee), card testing (bursts of tiny payments to check stolen card numbers, which lead to bigger losses later, counted as 350 each) and scam payments the customer is tricked into making (they look much like genuine payments, which is why rules struggle). Loan applications: 800 a day for 14 days, about 1.3% fraudulent. Synthetic identities are built from a mix of real and invented details: a young credit file padded with authorised-user accounts, often a phone or address shared with other applications and a new email. They borrow the most they can and never repay (95% of the loan lost). Stolen identities have older files but new emails.

Rules and alerts. A transaction or application raises an alert if any rule that's switched on matches it. Every genuine one flagged is a false alarm, with a friction cost: a genuine payment held (3) or a genuine applicant held up, some of whom walk away (40).

Your team. Capacity is analysts × alerts each can work in a day. Alerts are worked in the order they arrive; on a day with more alerts than capacity, only that share are worked in time, so the same share of real fraud among them is stopped. A fraud worked in time stops 90% of an account-takeover or card loss and 95% of a loan loss, but only 50% of a scam payment: tricked customers often insist the payment is genuine. Every alert worked costs the review cost you set.

Net benefit is fraud losses stopped, minus review cost, minus friction. Fit the rules to my team moves each switched-on rule, one at a time and twice over, to the setting with the highest net benefit that keeps the average day within capacity; it's a simple search, not the best possible answer. Your own rule is written by Claude, by Anthropic, as a maker and a checker; it can only use the fields listed, and every condition must be true for an alert.
